1Docking holes

Pricked, so it bakes flat.

Small holes pressed through the dough before baking let the steam out. Without them the biscuit would puff up and bake uneven.

2The stamp

A wheat ear and its name.

Pressed into the dough just before the oven, so it sets as it bakes and still shows on the finished biscuit.

3The crumb

Sandy, open, full of pores.

Wholemeal flour with flecks of bran and a little malt bakes into an open crumb, with thousands of tiny pores for the tea to climb.

The range

Nine digestives, one for the cup.

Same wholemeal base, different tops. Watch the one in the middle: it was ours all along.

From the cup to the mouth, in eight seconds.

ii. Dip

In it goes.

Wheat side down, about halfway in. The tea is around 70 °C, hot enough to soften the fat that holds the crumb together.

iii. Count · inside the cup

The soak.

Tea creeps into thousands of tiny pores and climbs above the surface. The wet front follows h² ∝ t, the Washburn equation: twice the height takes four times as long.

iv. Lift

Out, before it drops.

The wet half is heavier and weaker now. Keep it level and let it drip.

The soak follows Len Fisher's biscuit-dunking work (Nature, 1999), which used the Washburn equation for tea rising through a biscuit's pores. Flavour notes and dunk times are a matter of taste.
